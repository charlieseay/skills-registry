# Obviously Awesome — Situational Playbook

Use these patterns when you're stuck at a specific point in positioning or encounter a common challenge.

## Pattern: Customers Love You, But Prospects Don't Understand

**Situation:** You have happy customers but can't convert prospects. Prospects are confused about what you do or why they should care.

**Root cause:** Positioning problem (not marketing budget problem). You're either:
1. Targeting wrong segment
2. Positioning in the wrong category
3. Not clearly explaining your value vs. what prospects compare to

**How to solve:**
1. Review Step 7 (Target Market): Are prospects you're pitching actually your best-fit? If not, narrow to best-fit segment.
2. Review Step 8 (Market Category): Ask recent lost prospects how they'd categorize you. If different from your category, reconsider.
3. Review Step 6 (Value Proof): Is your value statement fact-based? Can you back it with customer proof?
4. Review Step 4 (Alternatives): Are you explaining your value relative to what prospects actually compare you to?

## Pattern: Long Sales Cycles and Low Close Rates

**Situation:** Sales takes 3+ months per deal, close rates are below 20%, sales team spends half the time explaining what you do.

**Root cause:** Positioning puts you in the wrong category or against the wrong alternatives.

**How to solve:**
1. **Step 4 (Alternatives):** Talk to 5-10 recent prospects who declined. Ask what they ended up using instead. If the answer is different from what you think, your competitive alternatives are wrong.
2. **Step 8 (Market Category):** Ask prospects how they'd categorize you independently. If their category doesn't match yours, repositioning could cut sales cycle in half.
3. **Step 7 (Target Segment):** Are you targeting the right buyers? Talk to your best customers about the buying decision—who was in the room? What was the selection process? Prospects taking 3 months to decide often aren't the right segment.

**Quick fix to test:** Reposition to a different category with the same core value in your next sales conversation. Record how the conversation flows. Better or worse? If better, you've found a repositioning opportunity.

## Pattern: High Churn Immediately After Purchase

**Situation:** Customers buy but churn within 3-6 months because they expected features you don't have or a different use case than you deliver.

**Root cause:** Positioning brought in wrong customers (customers buying for wrong reasons, not your target segment).

**How to solve:**
1. **Step 7 (Target Segment):** Review churned customers. Do they match your best-fit customer profile? If not, they weren't your target. Your positioning is attracting the wrong segment.
2. **Step 4 & 8 (Alternatives + Category):** What did churned customers think they were buying? It sounds like your category position triggered wrong assumptions. Reconsider category choice.
3. **Step 6 (Value):** What value did churned customers expect? If it's different from what you deliver, your value proposition isn't clear or you're reaching the wrong people.

**Investigation:** Exit interviews with churned customers (10+). Ask: "When you bought us, what problem were you trying to solve?" If the answer is different from your intended positioning, you have a positioning problem.

## Pattern: Losing to Competitors You Didn't Know Existed

**Situation:** Deal is going well until customer says "actually, we're also evaluating [competitor you've never heard of]" or chooses an alternative you didn't know was in the picture.

**Root cause:** You have the competitive alternatives wrong (Step 4). There's a competitor or alternative in customers' minds that isn't in yours.

**How to solve:**
1. **Step 4 (Alternatives):** In your next 5 customer conversations, ask: "What else did you consider?" and "Why didn't you choose [obvious competitor]?" Listen for alternatives you didn't know existed.
2. **Step 5-6 (Attributes + Value):** Once you find the hidden competitor, ask why customers compared you to them. What value did they think that competitor had? This reveals which of your attributes matter most.
3. **Step 8 (Market Category):** The hidden competitor may suggest a different market category than you're using. Example: You see yourself as "email," but customers compare you to "team chat." Different category entirely.

## Pattern: Product Roadmap Doesn't Align With Positioning

**Situation:** Positioning document says "we're a data warehouse for financial services," but product is building features for general analytics, healthcare compliance, etc.

**Root cause:** Positioning isn't actually strategic; it's just a document. Product team isn't using it to prioritize.

**How to solve:**
1. **Make positioning explicit in roadmap process:** Every epic/feature request gets asked: "Does this reinforce our positioning, drift from it, or stay neutral?" Rate features as "core to positioning," "adjacent," or "off strategy."
2. **Reposition if you must:** If product roadmap is genuinely taking you in a new direction (e.g., serving healthcare), that's okay—but reposition to match (Steps 1-9). Don't let product roadmap and positioning diverge silently.
3. **Product leadership alignment:** Does VP Product understand positioning? Are they involved in Step 2 (positioning team)? If not, they may see positioning as marketing's job, not strategy.

## Pattern: Your Positioning Is Backward Looking

**Situation:** You position based on what you built two years ago, but your product has evolved. Market has changed. Customers use you differently than you expected.

**Root cause:** Positioning baggage (Step 3) hasn't been revisited. Markets evolve faster than positioning updates.

**How to solve:**
1. **Revisit Step 1 (Best Customers):** Who are your best customers *now*? Might be different from three years ago. Build new best-fit list.
2. **Revisit Step 4 (Alternatives):** What do current best customers compare you to? Probably different from what you thought two years ago.
3. **Do a lightweight refresh:** You don't need to redo all 10 steps. But revisit 1, 4, 7, and 8 (customers, alternatives, segment, category) to see if anything has shifted.

**Timing:** Positioning should be reviewed 1-2 times per year, or whenever you notice:
- Customer acquisition profile is changing
- Sales cycle is significantly changing
- Market is shifting (new competitors, new trends)
- Best customers look different than they did 12 months ago

## Pattern: Everyone Interprets Positioning Differently

**Situation:** Sales is positioning one way ("we're the cheapest"), product thinks it's about features, marketing is talking about trends. No consistency.

**Root cause:** Positioning documentation is missing, vague, or not enforced.

**How to solve:**
1. **Document explicitly (Step 10):** Use the template: competitive alternatives, attributes, value (with proof), target segment, category, trends. Get specific. "Value is lower cost" isn't specific enough. "Saves enterprise customers $2M/year in licensing vs. Salesforce, with proof from 5 customer cases" is specific.
2. **Make reference easy:** One-page visual of positioning on Confluence/wiki that every employee can find in 10 seconds.
3. **Train on it:** New hire onboarding should include 20-minute walkthrough of positioning.
4. **Enforce in decisions:** In product reviews, ask "does this reinforce positioning?" In marketing planning, ask "what value and segment is this campaign hitting?"

## Pattern: You're Expanding Into a New Market

**Situation:** Your original positioning works in Market A. You want to reach Market B. Can you use the same positioning or do you need to reposition?

**How to solve:**
1. **Identify best customers in Market B.** Are they similar to Market A best customers? If yes, same positioning might work.
2. **If different segment in Market B:** Likely need to reposition. Run Steps 1-3 with Market B customers.
3. **Test before repositioning:** Talk to 10 Market B prospects. Ask what they'd do if you didn't exist (competitive alternatives). If different from Market A, you'll need different positioning.
4. **Consider subcategory positioning:** Instead of "we're CRM" for both markets, maybe "we're CRM for real estate" (Market A) and "CRM for nonprofits" (Market B).

**Example:** Data warehouse company positioned for "financial services/fraud detection" wants to enter healthcare market. Healthcare has different alternatives (legacy EHR analytics, legacy data warehouse vendors). Positioning for fraud detection doesn't resonate. Better to reposition as "data warehouse for patient outcome analytics" to hit the alternatives healthcare buyers actually consider.

## Pattern: Competitive Threat or New Entrant Copies Your Positioning

**Situation:** A well-funded competitor with better resources just repositioned to claim your market category and segment.

**Root cause:** You let them copy your position. This is common; incumbents often don't defend.

**How to solve:**
1. **Don't fight them in the same category:** If they're bigger, better-funded, or have more brand authority, matching positioning is losing.
2. **Reposition to a subcategory where you're stronger:** You claimed "data warehouse for fraud detection." They claimed "data warehouse." You retreat to "real-time data warehouse for financial crime" (more specific, smaller market, you own it).
3. **Build evidence and proof:** Once repositioned, build proof faster than they can: case studies, customer testimonials, analyst rankings in your new space.
4. **Own the segment:** Your smaller, more focused segment often has higher lifetime value and loyalty anyway.

## Pattern: Investors Want One Positioning; Customers Want Another

**Situation:** Investors want to hear you're building a $10B market category. Customers just want you to solve their problem.

**How to solve:**
This isn't a problem; it's normal. You can have two positionings:

1. **Investor positioning:** "We're building the financial services data warehouse category. $50B TAM, growing 25%/year. We're capturing 1% in 5 years = $500M revenue opportunity."

2. **Customer positioning:** "We're a real-time data warehouse for banks that need to detect fraud faster."

These don't contradict. Investors pitch talks about vision and market size. Customer materials talk about current solution and value.

**Key:** Never let investor positioning leak into customer-facing materials. Investors care about future opportunity. Customers care about solving today's problem. Use the right positioning for the audience.
