# Obviously Awesome — How to Nail Product Positioning

## Philosophy

**Positioning is context.** The book's central metaphor: just as the opening scene of a movie sets context that helps viewers understand everything that follows, product positioning sets context that helps customers understand your product's value. Without good context, even world-class products (like Joshua Bell playing violin in a subway) get lost or misunderstood.

**Positioning is deliberate, not accidental.** Most companies stumble into a default positioning based on what they set out to build, then never reconsider it even as the product or market evolves. Great positioning requires recognizing that every product can be positioned in multiple ways—and most products are better positioned differently than their default position.

**Components over statements.** Reject the traditional "positioning statement" exercise (which is backward and produces useless output). Instead, break positioning into six interconnected components and work through them systematically in the right order: starting with competitive alternatives customers see, then layering on unique attributes, value, target market, market category, and trends.

**Start with best-fit customers.** The 10-step process begins by identifying and deeply understanding your happiest customers—not guessing. They reveal what your product really is, who it naturally appeals to, and why positioning works differently than you assumed.

## The Six Components of Effective Positioning

| # | Component | Definition | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Competitive Alternatives** | What customers would do if your product didn't exist | Establish your true frame of reference—often not what you think (Excel, hire someone, do nothing—not other startups) |
| 2 | **Unique Attributes** | Features/capabilities you have that alternatives don't | Your secret sauce, the things you do better |
| 3 | **Value (and Proof)** | The real-world benefit customers get from those attributes | Why someone should care about your secret sauce (fact-based, not opinion) |
| 4 | **Target Market Characteristics** | Who cares most about your value | The customers who buy quickly, rarely haggle, and refer you |
| 5 | **Market Category** | The frame of reference you use to help customers understand your value | The shorthand that triggers powerful assumptions (CRM → Salesforce, email → features X/Y/Z) |
| 6 | **(Bonus) Relevant Trends** | Market movements your target customers understand and care about right now | Makes your product more urgent/strategic (e.g., "AI-powered CRM" not just "CRM") |

**Critical:** Components are interdependent and must be worked through in order. Attributes are only "unique" relative to specific alternatives. Value depends on attributes. Target market emerges from who cares about the value. Market category is chosen to highlight that value. Trends reinforce why it matters *now*.

## The book's throughline in one page

1. **Positioning is context, not category.** Customers use context clues—messaging, pricing, partners, features, branding—to make sense of new products. Bad context (violin player in subway station) ruins even world-class value. Good context makes value obvious.

2. **Most products are positioned wrong by default.** Because we know the product's history, we default-position it as what we set out to build. Customers have no such baggage and often discover the product is better framed differently (cake → muffin → cake pop; database → data warehouse; email for lawyers → team collaboration software).

3. **Start with best-fit customers, not features.** The happiest customers reveal what your product actually is and what really matters. They eliminate the guesswork. This is where every positioning effort should begin.

4. **Work through the six components in order.** Competitive alternatives → attributes → value → target market → market category → trends. Each component depends on the previous one. Skip this sequence and positioning falls apart.

5. **Market category is powerful and dangerous.** Declare yourself a "CRM" and customers assume Salesforce is your competitor, that you have calendar/email/activity tracking, and that you cost similar money. This works *for* you if you choose wisely. It works *against* you if customers' assumptions don't match your reality.

6. **Positioning statements are the wrong tool.** They're backward (assume you know the answers before researching), unactionable (no team uses them), and hard to remember. Replace them with systematic component work that can actually inform product strategy, messaging, hiring, and roadmaps.

## When to use this skill (situational index)

- **You have a product customers love but prospects don't understand it** → Start at Step 1 (understand best customers) and work through all 10
- **New product launch; positioning from scratch** → Begin at Step 1; assume wide market, narrow down as patterns emerge
- **Sales cycles are long and close rates are low** → Likely positioning problem; Steps 7-8 (target market + market category) often fix this
- **Customers churn quickly after purchase, expecting features you didn't promise** → Step 7 targeting issue; wrong people bought you for wrong reasons
- **You're losing to competitors you didn't know about** → Step 4 (competitive alternatives); customers see a different competitive set than you do
- **You want to expand into a new market or customer segment** → Use Steps 1-3 to surface baggage, then Steps 4-8 to reposition for the new segment
- **Messaging is failing despite solid product** → Likely Steps 5-6 issue (attributes not clearly mapped to customer value)
- **Market is shifting around you (technology, trends, new competitors)** → Steps 3 and 9 (baggage, trends); you may need to reposition even though product didn't change
