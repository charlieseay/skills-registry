# Step 3: Align Positioning Vocabulary and Let Go of Positioning Baggage

## Core Idea
Before your team can discover new positioning, they must speak the same language and consciously set aside the old ways they've been thinking about the product. "Positioning baggage" is the accumulated history and assumptions baked into your current positioning. You can't see alternatives if you're stuck defending the status quo.

## Common Positioning Vocabulary

Ensure your team agrees on:
- **What positioning means:** Context-setting for customers, not just messaging or category. How customers come to understand your product within its best frame of reference.
- **Why it matters:** Every marketing and sales tactic depends on positioning. Weak positioning makes everything harder. Strong positioning makes everything easier.
- **The six components:** Competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value, target market characteristics, market category, relevant trends—and how they interconnect.
- **Why positioning statements are dangerous:** They assume you already know the answers, reinforce status quo, produce unactionable output, and get filed away unused.

Spend the first hour of your positioning workshop on common definitions so you're not arguing about what positioning is instead of doing positioning.

## What Is Positioning Baggage?

Positioning baggage is your company's history baked into how you see the product. Examples:

- You built it as "Email software for lawyers" and now you're stuck seeing it as email, even though customers actually value you for team collaboration and file sharing (the collaboration frame makes your value more obvious).
- Your product started as a "database" but over time evolved into an analytics platform. You still call it a database because that's what you set out to build. Customers see it differently.
- You imagined a product for "accountants" but discovered your best customers are "small business owners who hire bookkeepers." You're still messaging to accountants because that was your original vision.

**Customers don't carry this baggage.** They encounter your product with no history. What seems obvious to you (because you know the history) looks confusing or poorly conceived to them.

## Freeing Your Mind

Steps to surface and release positioning baggage:

1. **Name where history appears in your current positioning.** Did you use terminology from launch day? Are you still targeting the market from your original vision? Are there features described the way you designed them rather than how customers value them?

2. **Create team awareness that history isn't destiny.** Arm & Hammer started as baking soda for baking. In the 1970s, as baking declined but odor control grew, they let go of "baking" positioning and repositioned around deodorizing. The product didn't change; the context did. Revenue went from $16M (1969) to $318M (1987).

3. **Get explicit team agreement to suspend judgment.** Although the product was created with a certain market and audience in mind, agree that it may no longer be best positioned that way. Suspend old opinions for the duration of the exercise and remain open to new ideas.

4. **Watch for generational baggage.** Founders and long-tenured employees carry the full history. Newer hires often see the product more clearly because they have no baggage. Explicitly draw this out: newer employees often see positioning opportunities the founders miss.

5. **Reframe as discovery, not betrayal.** Letting go of baggage isn't saying "we were wrong." It's saying "we learned something new about the market and we're adjusting." Apple positioned the iPhone as "an internet communicator," but customers wanted it as a mobile phone. Steve Jobs had to let go of "communicator" to see what customers really wanted.

## Market Maturity vs. Competitive Landscape

The type of positioning you choose depends on where your market is:

**Early-stage market (new problem, few solutions):**
- Positioning baggage is least damaging because customers have no frame of reference anyway
- Your job is to educate the market about the problem, not compete on differentiation
- Positioning focuses on the problem and the solution, not how you're different
- *Example:* Slack early on had to position around "eliminating email overload" because the "chat for teams" category didn't exist yet

**Mature market with established categories:**
- Positioning baggage is most damaging because customers have strong assumptions about what categories mean
- Your job is to find a category or reframe where your strengths become obvious vs. incumbents
- Positioning focuses on differentiators and why you're better in a specific segment
- *Example:* Clearpath Robotics repositioned from "industrial robots" to "autonomous vehicles for manufacturing" to escape direct comparison with stationary robots

**Crowded market with many competitors:**
- Positioning baggage causes you to compete on the same dimensions as everyone else
- Your job is to find a different frame where different attributes matter
- Positioning often requires finding an underserved customer segment or market category
- *Example:* A database company (Dunford's) repositioned as a data warehouse to escape comparison with general-purpose databases

## Red Flags

- **Team is defensive about current positioning:** Baggage is running deep. Spend more time on this step.
- **Founders/executives keep saying "but we built it as...":** This is baggage speaking. Acknowledge it, document it, then set it aside.
- **Team jumps to "we're different because of X feature":** Feature comparisons are baggage. Focus on customer value first.
- **Long silence when you ask "what if we positioned this completely differently?":** Baggage is blocking creative thinking. Push on it.
