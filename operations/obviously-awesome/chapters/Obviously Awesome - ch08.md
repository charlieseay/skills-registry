# Step 8: Find Market Frame of Reference and Position Within It

## Core Idea
Market category is a frame of reference that triggers powerful assumptions in customers' minds. Declare yourself a "CRM" and customers assume Salesforce is your competitor, that you have contact management/activity tracking, that pricing is per-user monthly. This works *for* you if your category choice is wise; it works *against* you if customers' assumptions don't match your reality. This step is about finding the best category where your unique strengths become obvious to your target segment.

## How Market Categories Work

When customers encounter a new product, they need to make sense of it. They do this by fitting it into a category they already understand.

**Example: iPad (2010)**
Customer initially thought: "That's like a big iPhone" or "That's like a computer without a keyboard."
Apple positioned it as: "Post-PC device" (new category)
The category helped customers understand what the product was for and why.

**Example: Uber (2010)**
Customer initially thought: "That's like a taxi company but with an app"
Uber positioned it as: "Ride-sharing" (implying peer-to-peer, not traditional taxi)
The category helped customers understand the business model and pricing structure.

When you declare a market category, customers assume:
- **Who your competitors are** (category leaders/alternatives)
- **What features you'll have** (basic category functionality)
- **What pricing model you use** (category norms)
- **What problem you solve** (the category's core problem)

## The Power of Category Choice

**If you choose wisely:** All assumptions work for you. You don't have to explain who your competitors are; it's assumed. You don't have to list every feature; category norms are assumed.

**If you choose poorly:** Customers' assumptions conflict with your reality, and you waste energy fighting assumptions instead of selling the value.

**Example: Email for Lawyers**
Company built tool for secure file sharing and collaboration. They positioned it as "email for lawyers" because they wanted to replace email for their workflow. But:
- Customers expected calendar, contacts, activity tracking (standard email features) — the tool didn't have them
- Customers' key need was file sharing; email customers see that as a secondary feature
- Positioning as "email" made their strength (collaboration, file sharing) seem like a limitation

Repositioning as "team collaboration software" made the value obvious and eliminated assumption conflicts.

## How to Choose Your Market Category

**1. Start with your value themes and target segment (Steps 6-7).**

Which category (or categories) would naturally be associated with that value?

Example: If your value is "get insights from data faster" and your segment is "financial services," potential categories:
- Business intelligence
- Data warehouse
- Data analytics platform
- Risk management software

**2. For each potential category, ask:**
- Do customers in my target segment already understand this category?
- Does this category trigger assumptions about features I actually have?
- Does this category trigger assumptions about competitors that are actually my competitors (in customers' minds)?
- Does this category put my strengths at the center or hide them?

**3. Map each category to the assumptions it triggers:**

| Category | Assumed Competitors | Assumed Features | Assumed Pricing | Fit for Us? |
|----------|-------------------|-----------------|-----------------|-----------|
| Business Intelligence | Tableau, Power BI, Looker | Dashboards, ad-hoc queries, connectors | Per-user or per-connection | Good—we're analytics-focused |
| Database | PostgreSQL, Oracle, SQL Server | DDL, transactions, backups | Per-license, per-core | Bad—customers expect general-purpose features we don't have |
| Data Warehouse | Snowflake, Redshift, BigQuery | Structured data, SQL, scale | Per-compute or per-storage | Good—we're optimized for analytics at scale |

The category that aligns your actual strengths with customer assumptions is your best choice.

## Repositioning Stories

**Database to Data Warehouse** (Dunford's example)
- Old positioning triggered wrong assumptions (general-purpose database features, competition with incumbents who owned the space)
- New positioning (data warehouse) aligned customer assumptions with actual product (analytics optimized, competing against specific alternatives like Tableau + homegrown solutions)
- Result: Shorter sales cycles, faster closes, investor interest

**Clearpath Robotics: Robot to Autonomous Vehicle**
- Old positioning: "industrial robot" (customers assumed stationary, repetitive-task machines)
- Reality: Mobile, intelligent, dispatch-managed fleet
- New positioning: "autonomous vehicles for industrial use" (better aligned with actual capabilities)
- Result: Sales acceleration, $30M Series B, market leadership

**Sampler: Online Sampling to Direct-to-Consumer Sampling**
- Old positioning: "online product sampling" (confused whether they handled physical samples)
- Reality: Helping brands build owned customer relationships and gather data
- New positioning: "direct-to-consumer sampling" (aligned with customer's strategic initiative)
- Result: Brands made Sampler a strategic priority, deals closed faster

## Finding Underserved Categories

Sometimes your strength becomes obvious not in an existing category but in a category that's underserved or newly emerging.

**Key insight:** In a mature, crowded market, the incumbent category leader wins. But if you can find a subcategory where you're uniquely strong, positioning there gives you advantage.

Example: Instead of "CRM" (Salesforce owns it), position as:
- "CRM for real estate agents"
- "CRM for consulting firms"
- "CRM for nonprofits"

The subcategory positioning lets you compete on specific strengths vs. that segment's needs, not general features vs. the category leader.

## Deciding: Should You Position in an Existing Category or Create a New One?

**Position in existing category if:**
- The category is mature and your target segment already understands it
- You can claim clear leadership or differentiation within it
- Your strengths align with category assumptions
- The category leader is weak or doesn't own the segment you want

**Create/define new category if:**
- No existing category captures your value clearly
- Existing categories trigger wrong assumptions
- You're pioneering a new market (e.g., "cloud accounting" didn't exist before Wave)
- You want to own a new space before competitors commoditize it

Creating a new category is harder (customers don't have mental model) but can be worth it if you're truly innovative.

## Red Flags

- **Customers keep saying "it's like [other product]" even after you explain.** Your category isn't clicking. Choose a different one.
- **Sales has to spend half the pitch explaining what category you're in.** Category is wrong; choose one that's more intuitive.
- **You're positioned in a category dominated by a much larger competitor** and you can't differentiate. Consider repositioning in a subcategory.
- **Your messaging contradicts your category.** You say "we're CRM" but describe features that sound like "operations platform." Pick one.
- **Best-fit customers don't recognize themselves in the category.** You're not reaching them. Refine the category definition.
