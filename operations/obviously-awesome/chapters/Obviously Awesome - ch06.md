# Step 6: Map Attributes to Value Themes

## Core Idea
Unique attributes are meaningless without value. If you have a patented algorithm, so what? The value is what that algorithm enables for customers—faster decisions, more accurate results, less time spent. Map each attribute to the real-world customer benefit it delivers. Value must be fact-based and provable; customer opinion, not your opinion, counts as proof.

## The Difference Between Attributes and Value

- **Attribute:** "We have a patented query algorithm"
- **Value:** "Our algorithm lets you get answers from your data in minutes instead of hours, so your teams can make faster decisions"

- **Attribute:** "Mobile app"
- **Value:** "Sales reps can update deals from the field without returning to the office, so you don't miss pipeline visibility"

- **Attribute:** "HIPAA compliance"
- **Value:** "You can store patient data without legal risk, so you can scale your medical practice"

Every attribute must map to at least one value statement. If you can't explain the customer benefit, either the attribute doesn't belong or you don't fully understand why you built it.

## How to Map Attributes to Value

For each unique attribute (Step 5), ask:

1. **What does this enable the customer to do that they couldn't do before or couldn't do as well?**
   - Faster → decision-making, time-to-market, responsiveness
   - Easier → adoption, training, support costs, user satisfaction
   - Better accuracy → reduced errors, compliance, risk reduction
   - More data/visibility → better insights, intelligence, strategy

2. **Who benefits most?** (This matters for Step 7; note it)
   - Database admins
   - VP of Sales
   - Finance teams
   - Line-of-business managers

3. **What's the proof that this value is real?**
   - Customer testimonial ("We now close deals 40% faster")
   - Data ("95% of users report faster reporting, 60% report better decisions")
   - Third-party review or benchmark
   - Observable outcome ("We moved from monthly to weekly forecasting")

## The Critical Rule: Fact-Based, Not Opinion-Based

This is where April is most emphatic: **your opinion of the value doesn't count. Customer, reviewer, or data-based proof does.**

Bad value statements (opinion-based):
- "Beautifully designed user interface" (subjective; who decides "beautiful"?)
- "The best solution in the market" (self-serving)
- "Incredibly powerful" (vague; powerful for what?)
- "Loved by our customers" (says what, specifically?)

Good value statements (fact-based):
- "Reduces data entry time by 60% vs. spreadsheets" (data)
- "Supports regulatory compliance for HIPAA-covered entities" (objective requirement met)
- "Quarterly NPS of 72, vs. industry average of 45" (third-party data)
- "3/5 of our B2B customers expanded to additional business units within 12 months" (observable outcome)
- "Salesforce users who added our tool to their stack reported 25% faster deal cycles" (customer data)

## Finding Proof

Look for evidence in:
- **Customer testimonials:** "Before this tool, I spent 2 hours daily on manual reporting. Now it's 15 minutes."
- **Quantitative outcomes:** Sales cycle reduction, error rate decrease, time savings, revenue growth
- **Third-party validation:** Industry analysts, G2 reviews, case studies, press coverage
- **Awards or certifications:** "SOC 2 certified," "Gartner Magic Quadrant," "Built with AWS"
- **Customer logos and scale:** "Trusted by 5,000+ companies including [big names]"
- **Behavioral proof:** Retention rates, expansion rates, referral rates show the value is real

## Building Your Value Theme Map

Create a simple table:

| Attribute | Value to Customers | Proof | Beneficiary Role |
|-----------|-------------------|-------|------------------|
| Patented algorithm | Get data answers in minutes, not hours | "Typical query now runs 200ms vs. 20 seconds with old platform" (customer quote) | Data analysts, BI teams |
| Mobile app | Sales team updates deals from field without returning to office | "80% of our sales team said they now log deals same day vs. end-of-week" (customer research) | Sales reps, sales managers |
| HIPAA compliance | Store patient data without legal risk | "SOC 2 Type II certified; passed all audits with no exceptions" (third-party) | Compliance officers, practice owners |

Multiple attributes can map to the same value. Multiple values can come from one attribute. The goal is clarity: everyone understands what problem this solves for whom.

## Red Flags

- **Value is generic or could apply to any product in your category.** "Easy to use," "saves time," "improves productivity"—these apply to everything. Be specific.
- **You list an attribute but can't articulate why customers care.** You might not understand why that feature exists. Consider removing it or learning more.
- **Proof is missing or vague.** "Customers love it" isn't proof. What specific customers said what?
- **Value doesn't connect to competitive alternatives.** You claimed attribute X is unique vs. spreadsheets, but your value statement doesn't explain why that matters vs. spreadsheets specifically.
- **Too many value themes from one attribute.** Focus. One attribute might have 2-3 value themes maximum. If more, you're confusing attributes.
