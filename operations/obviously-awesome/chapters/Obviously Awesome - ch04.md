# Step 4: List Your True Competitive Alternatives

## Core Idea
Customers don't always see competitors the way you do—and their perception is the only one that matters for positioning. The competitive alternative is what customers would do if your product didn't exist. This is often not another product but a process, spreadsheet, manual workaround, or doing nothing entirely. Your unique attributes only matter relative to the alternatives customers actually consider.

## Why Most Companies Get This Wrong

Product teams typically look at:
- Competitors fighting for the same funding/market position
- Direct product alternatives (other SaaS tools, other databases, etc.)
- Market analysts' competitive matrices

Customers look at:
- What they use today
- What they've heard of
- What solves their immediate problem right now

These are often completely different. A database company's product team might worry about PostgreSQL and other startups. But the actual competitive set in customers' minds might be: "Use our existing database," "Use a data warehouse vendor," or "Build it ourselves with tools we have."

## How to Identify True Competitive Alternatives

**Ask your best-fit customers:** "What would you do if our product didn't exist?"

Listen carefully for:
- **Direct product competitors** (sometimes, but often not your top concern)
- **Product category alternatives** ("We'd use spreadsheets and manual work")
- **Process alternatives** ("We'd hire an intern," "We'd use a consultant")
- **Status quo alternatives** ("We'd do it ourselves with what we have," "We'd do nothing and accept the pain")

The most common competitive alternative for business software is: **spreadsheets, documents, presentations, and manual processes combined with internal labor.**

## Examples of Non-Product Alternatives

- "Hire someone to do it manually"
- "Use Excel and Google Sheets"
- "Build it ourselves"
- "Do nothing and live with the problem"
- "Hire a consultant or agency"
- "Use a general-purpose tool (Notion, Airtable, Zapier) and configure it ourselves"

These are often your real competitors, not the startups you worry about.

## Creating Your Alternatives List

1. **Start with your best-fit customers** (from Step 1). Ask what they would do if you didn't exist.
2. **Remain focused on what those customers see as alternatives.** Edge-case customers might name obscure solutions; focus on what the majority would really do.
3. **Rank from most common to least common.** You're trying to focus on what most customers would actually do, not rare scenarios.
4. **Group similar alternatives if they cluster naturally:**
   - "Do it manually" group: hire intern, do it myself, spreadsheet + manual work
   - "Use existing vendor" group: Salesforce, HubSpot, etc.
   - "Use a category solution" group: data warehouse vendors, BI tools, etc.

**Result:** Teams usually end up with 2-5 groups of alternatives.

## What NOT to Include

- **Tiny competitors with no market traction:** If a competitor has almost no customers, your target customers don't see them as an alternative. They don't have mindshare.
- **Your startup competitors:** Unless customers are genuinely aware of them, they're not part of the customer's competitive landscape. Investors see you as competing with other Series A startups; customers don't.
- **Hypothetical alternatives:** Focus on what customers actually do, not what theoretically exists.
- **Your own product positioned differently:** The alternative is what they use without you, not another version of your product.

## Why This Matters for Every Other Step

Once you define competitive alternatives, everything else follows:
- **Unique attributes** (Step 5): You can only claim something is unique if the alternatives don't have it
- **Value** (Step 6): Your value is how you're better than these specific alternatives, not in some absolute sense
- **Target market** (Step 7): The customer segment that cares most about your advantage over these alternatives
- **Market category** (Step 8): The frame that makes your strengths vs. these alternatives most obvious

Get this wrong and every step that follows falls apart.

## Story: Database Company's Customer Eyes

April's database startup thought their competitive set was:
- PostgreSQL (big open-source database)
- Oracle (big commercial database)
- Competing startups (other database innovations)

When they asked customers what they would do if the database didn't exist, the answers were:
- "Use a business intelligence tool"
- "Use a data warehouse"
- "Build our own analytics on top of our existing database"

Customers weren't comparing the startup database to PostgreSQL at all. They were comparing it to business intelligence and data warehousing solutions. The startup was positioned wrong because they had the competitive set wrong.

## Red Flags

- **List is very long (7+):** You're either not focused on best-fit customers or you're including too many rare alternatives.
- **List is full of company names, no processes:** Customers have more varied alternatives than "other companies." Make sure you're hearing about spreadsheets, manual work, status quo, doing nothing.
- **Team is defensive about leaving competitors off:** If the competitor isn't in your customers' minds, it doesn't belong on the list—regardless of its existence in the market.
- **List includes "everyone" or "no one in particular":** You've lost focus on best-fit customers. Return to Step 1.
