# Step 10: Capture Your Positioning So It Can Be Shared

## Core Idea
Positioning is only useful if the entire company understands it and uses it to inform decisions. Step 10 is about documenting your positioning in a format that's easy to reference, impossible to misinterpret, and actionable across every team in your organization. A positioning statement buried in a deck is useless; positioning woven into messaging, roadmaps, hiring, and strategy is how you win.

## Why Documentation Matters

A positioning workshop can be inspiring and create temporary alignment. But the moment the workshop ends and people return to their silos, positioning gets reinterpreted or forgotten. The first salesperson who didn't attend the workshop will make up their own positioning. The first product decision that hasn't been explicitly tied to positioning will drift.

Documentation forces clarity. When you write down your positioning, you find gaps and conflicts. You can't hand-wave through them.

## What to Document

**1. The Six Components (Explicit and Detailed)**

Write out each component so there's zero ambiguity:

**Competitive Alternatives:**
- List each group of alternatives
- For each, describe what customers would do if you didn't exist
- Rank by frequency (most common first)
- Example: "Vs. spreadsheets and manual work: 60% of best-fit customers do this today; vs. legacy data warehouse: 30%; vs. specialized BI tool: 10%"

**Unique Attributes:**
- List each attribute
- Explain why it matters relative to competitive alternatives
- Avoid generic feature-tour language
- Example: "Our patented query algorithm enables 100x faster data retrieval than SQL queries on traditional databases. This matters most when competing against: (a) spreadsheets requiring manual aggregation, (b) legacy data warehouses with query latency measured in minutes."

**Value Themes:**
- List each value with proof
- Reference which attributes enable it
- Note which customer segment values it most
- Example: "Financial analysts can generate monthly reports in 5 minutes vs. 2 hours with legacy warehouse. Proof: Quantitative benchmark test; three customer testimonials ('We moved from weekly to daily reporting'); case study comparing query times. Most valued by: Financial services segment, analysts who run reports 10+ times per week."

**Target Market Characteristics:**
- Describe the segment in detail
- What sets them apart from other customers who use your product?
- How are they identified in the market? (job titles, industries, company sizes, keywords, etc.)
- Example: "Banks and financial services companies with (a) more than $1B in assets under management, (b) need to do real-time fraud detection or risk analysis, (c) currently using Excel + manual processes or legacy data warehouses. Identifiable by: job titles (Chief Analytics Officer, VP of Risk, Data Analytics Director); company size (typically 100+ employees); industry classification (NAICS 52—Financial and Insurance)."

**Market Category:**
- Explain which category you occupy
- Why this category (not alternatives)?
- What assumptions does it trigger? Are they accurate for you?
- Example: "We position as a 'data warehouse' (not 'database' or 'business intelligence'). We chose this because it aligns customer assumptions (built for analytics, not transactional workloads; structured data; SQL-based queries) with our actual product. Customers assume we compete with Redshift/Snowflake/BigQuery, which we do; they assume we're optimized for analytics, which we are; they don't assume we handle general-purpose database workloads, which is accurate."

**Relevant Trends:**
- State the trend explicitly
- Explain why it matters to your segment
- How does it connect to your positioning?
- Is it temporary or lasting?
- Example: "Trend: Real-time analytics is becoming table stakes, not a differentiator. More companies are moving from batch processing to streaming/real-time. This matters to our segment because fraud detection and risk analysis require real-time data. Our positioning as 'real-time analytics warehouse' connects to this trend by making our solution feel current and strategic. Likely duration: 3-5 years until real-time is assumed for all platforms."

**2. The Positioning Statement (But Not the Traditional Kind)**

April hates traditional positioning statements, but a concise summary is useful:

**Format (simple and memorable):**
"We are the [category] for [target customer] who [key need/value]. Unlike [alternative], we [key differentiator]. Relevant to them because [trend or urgency]."

**Example:**
"We are the real-time analytics warehouse for financial services companies who need to detect fraud and risk anomalies faster than competitors. Unlike legacy data warehouses that require hours to run complex queries, we return answers in minutes. Relevant now because financial institutions are competing on speed, and those who detect threats faster win."

This is not a marketing line; it's an internal clarity statement. It's short enough to remember and detailed enough to be unambiguous.

**3. How Positioning Connects to Every Function**

Write out what each team does with positioning:

**Marketing:**
- Messaging strategy (talk about this value to this segment in this category)
- Campaign targeting (reach this segment with this positioning)
- Content strategy (write case studies about this value, not other values)
- Website/brand positioning (what headline, imagery, copy reflects this positioning?)

**Sales:**
- Target account list (focus on companies matching these characteristics)
- Sales pitch deck structure (open with problem/alternative, show your unique solution, close with value/proof)
- Objection handling (when prospects name different alternatives or values, how do we respond?)
- Sales training (all reps use consistent positioning and language)

**Product:**
- Roadmap priority (features that reinforce this positioning rank higher)
- Feature development rationale (why are we building this? Because it strengthens our position in this market with this value)
- Deprecation decisions (features that don't serve this positioning get deprecated)
- Product design focus (where should UX/design effort go to strengthen positioning?)

**Customer Success:**
- Onboarding strategy (help customers realize this specific value, not generic features)
- Expansion strategy (once they see this value, what adjacent value can we show them?)
- Retention risk (customers who aren't getting this value are at risk)
- Reference program (ask customers who've seen this value to speak about it)

**Hiring:**
- Job descriptions (hire for the skills and perspective we need to execute this positioning)
- Interview messaging (talk about what we're trying to do in the market)
- Cultural reinforcement (new employees should understand our market position and strategy)

## Formats for Positioning Documentation

**Option 1: Positioning Brief (2-3 pages)**
- One-page summary (concise positioning statement + the six components in table form)
- One page on functional implications (what marketing/sales/product/success do with this)
- One page on FAQs and edge cases ("What about customers who don't fit this? How do we handle them?")

**Option 2: Positioning Playbook (longer, more detailed)**
- Full documentation of each component
- Competitive alternative matrix (our positioning vs. each competitor, visual)
- Value theme stories (for each value, detailed customer story showing the value)
- Sales frameworks and objection handling
- Marketing guidelines and examples
- Product strategy implications

**Option 3: Living Positioning Document**
- Stored in wiki or shared docs (not a PDF that gets stale)
- Updated quarterly or when market conditions change
- Comments and questions tracked
- Version history so teams know what changed and why

## Distribution and Adoption

After you document:

1. **Share with the team.** Walk through it in a meeting. Answer questions. Get agreement.
2. **Reference it constantly.** In product reviews, sales training, marketing planning, hiring.
3. **Revise it when you learn new information.** Positioning isn't static. As you learn about customers, markets, and competition, update it.
4. **Make it required reading for new employees.** Every hire should understand your positioning in their first week.
5. **Measure execution against it.** Are marketing messages aligned? Are sales conversations hitting the right value? Is product building features that reinforce positioning?

## Red Flags (Documentation Issues)

- **Positioning document is 30+ pages:** Too detailed to be useful. Cut to essentials.
- **Different versions exist in different departments.** Create one source of truth.
- **Document was created 2+ years ago and never updated.** Markets and companies evolve; positioning gets stale.
- **Employees can't articulate your positioning.** It's not internalized. Make it more accessible or spend more time on adoption.
- **Sales ignores the positioning and uses their own pitch.** Positioning isn't credible or isn't being enforced.
- **Product roadmap doesn't reflect positioning priorities.** Positioning is strategic statement, not actual strategy. Link them.

## What Happens After Positioning Is Documented

Positioning is the foundation for almost every other decision:

- **Marketing campaigns** are built to reach the target segment with the key value
- **Sales training** focuses reps on best-fit customers and key value themes
- **Product roadmap** prioritizes features that strengthen your positioning
- **Hiring** focuses on candidates who can execute this positioning
- **Partnerships** are chosen to strengthen this positioning
- **Pricing** reflects the value and segment you're targeting
- **Customer success playbooks** are designed around this value
- **Brand strategy** visuals, tone, and messaging align to this positioning

If positioning isn't woven into these decisions, you've done the work but haven't shipped the benefits. Go to Step 10 seriously.
