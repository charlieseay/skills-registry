# Step 7: Determine Who Cares a Lot

## Core Idea
Your value themes matter to some customers more than others. Your target market is the segment that cares *a lot* about the value you deliver—they buy quickly, rarely haggle on price, stay long-term, and refer you. Identifying this segment is critical because every other step depends on it: market category (Step 8) and trend positioning (Step 9) are chosen to reach this specific group.

## The Core Question

**Among customers who could benefit from your product, which segment benefits most and cares the most?**

Not every customer who could use your product will care equally. A project management tool is useful to almost any organization. But construction companies care about it more than consulting firms. A data warehouse is useful to many companies, but companies with massive datasets and time-sensitive analysis needs care about it more.

## How to Identify Your Target Segment

Start with your value themes (Step 6). For each value, ask:

1. **Who experiences this problem most acutely?**
   - If your value is "faster reporting," who needs reports most urgently? Finance teams updating monthly forecasts, sales ops tracking pipeline weekly, etc.
   - If your value is "easier invoicing," who sends invoices most frequently? Consultants, small agencies, freelancers—not once-a-year invoice senders.

2. **What characteristics distinguish this segment?**
   - **Frequency of need:** How often do they need this value? (Weekly/daily = higher care than monthly/quarterly)
   - **Scale of pain:** How much does this problem cost them or hurt them? (Missing a security threat = high pain; minor reporting delay = low pain)
   - **Competitive risk:** What competitive disadvantage do they face without this? (Sales reps without real-time pipeline = losing deals; admin without automated timesheet = minor annoyance)
   - **Priority in their organization:** Is this a strategic initiative or a nice-to-have?

3. **What can you observe about them?**
   - Industry/vertical: Healthcare, Financial services, Manufacturing, etc.
   - Company size: Startups, mid-market, enterprise
   - Role/function: VP of Sales, Data analysts, Finance controllers
   - Business type: B2B, B2C, marketplace, etc.
   - Growth stage: High-growth (need scaling solutions), mature/stable (need efficiency)
   - Geographic: Specific regions where your value matters most

4. **How can you identify them in the market?** (This matters for sales and marketing)
   - Job title searches
   - LinkedIn targeting
   - Industry publications they read
   - Events they attend
   - Trade associations they join
   - Keywords they search

## The Best-Fit Customer Characteristics

Your best-fit customers share attributes beyond "they use your product." Ask about your best customers (from Step 1):
- Do they all come from the same industry?
- Are they all similar company sizes?
- Do they all serve a similar end-market or customer type?
- Do they have a common job title or function?
- What other products do they use?
- How did they find you?
- What triggered their need for you?

A pattern will emerge. That pattern is your target market.

## Stories: Segmentation Shifts Positioning

**Dunford's Database Example**
- Early assumption: "Any company with lots of data"
- Reality: Companies storing data for backup don't need fast analysis; they don't care about speed
- Refined segment: Banks that need to detect fraud in real-time
- Shift: Suddenly clear who to target (banks), why they care (speed matters for security), and what competitive category made sense (data warehouse, not general database)

**Sampler (Product Sampling)**
- Early positioning: "Online product sampling"
- Customer confusion: "Do you handle physical samples or digital?"
- Repositioning insight: The customers who care most are brands building direct-to-consumer strategies
- Refined segment: Consumer brands (cosmetics, food, household goods) threatened by Amazon and direct competitors, investing in owned customer relationships
- Result: Clear messaging ("direct-to-consumer sampling"), faster deals, strategic positioning vs. tactical

## Red Flags

- **Segment is "everyone" or "any company":** You don't have true positioning yet. Narrow further.
- **You can't explain why this segment cares more than others.** Return to value themes (Step 6). Maybe they don't actually care that much about your value.
- **Your best customers don't fit the segment you defined.** Your definition is wrong or your best customers are from a different segment than you think. Revisit this step.
- **Segment is defined by product adoption (e.g., "companies using Salesforce")** rather than customer characteristics.** That's an audience, not a market. What about them makes them a fit for your value?
- **No clear way to reach this segment in the market.** If you can't identify them by job title, industry, size, or behavior, you'll struggle to market and sell to them.
