# Obviously Awesome — Glossary of Key Concepts

## Core Positioning Concepts

**Positioning**
The act of deliberately defining how you are the best at something that a defined market cares a lot about. More broadly: context-setting for your product that helps customers understand what it is, why it matters, and who it's for.

**Positioning Baggage**
The accumulated history and assumptions baked into how you think about your product. Founders and long-time employees carry positioning baggage; newer employees often see the product more clearly. Example: "We built it as a database for databases, so we still see it as a database" even though customers see it as a data warehouse.

**Context (Context-Setting)**
The clues—messaging, pricing, branding, features, partners, customers—that help customers make sense of a new product. A world-class product in the wrong context (Joshua Bell playing violin in a subway station) fails. The same product in the right context (concert hall) succeeds.

**Default Positioning**
The positioning you fall into unintentionally because you think there's only one way your product can be positioned. Defaults usually match what you set out to build, not what customers actually need or understand. Breaking out of default positioning requires deliberate re-thinking.

**Market Frame of Reference**
The lens through which customers understand your product. Examples: "I'm a CRM" triggers the frame "contact management software, like Salesforce." "I'm a data warehouse" triggers "analytics platform, like Snowflake." Choosing your frame carefully determines what customers assume about you.

## The Six Components

**Competitive Alternatives**
What customers would do if your product didn't exist. Often not other products but processes: "use spreadsheets," "hire an intern," "do it manually," "do nothing." Customers' perception of alternatives determines how they evaluate your product.

**Unique Attributes**
Capabilities, features, or characteristics you have that alternatives don't. Technical (patented algorithm), operational (on-premise delivery), or organizational (expertise). Attributes are only "unique" when compared to specific competitive alternatives.

**Value (and Proof)**
The real-world customer benefit enabled by your unique attributes. Fact-based, not opinion-based. Proof comes from customer testimonials, quantitative data, third-party validation, or observable outcomes—not from your assertion.

**Value Themes**
Clusters of related values. Example: "Faster analysis" and "more accurate forecasts" might both come from the same attribute (better algorithm) and roll up into the theme "Better Business Intelligence."

**Target Market Characteristics**
Who cares *a lot* about your value. Not everyone who could benefit, but the segment that benefits most, buys quickest, refers you, and stays longest. Described by industry, company size, role, company stage, or observable behaviors.

**Market Category**
The frame of reference you use to help customers understand what you are. Example: CRM, data warehouse, project management, team collaboration. Triggers powerful assumptions (feature sets, competitors, pricing). Can be existing (position within CRM) or new (create "direct-to-consumer sampling" category).

**Relevant Trends**
Market movements or shifts your target segment understands and cares about. AI, remote-first, direct-to-consumer, real-time analytics. Makes your product feel urgent and strategic right now. Not a substitute for market category but a secondary layer of context.

## Positioning Antipatterns

**Positioning Statement**
The traditional "FOR [target] our [product] IS A [category] WHICH [value] UNLIKE [competitor]" template. Dangerous because it assumes you already know the answers and reinforces status quo thinking. Replace with systematic component work instead.

**Feature-First Positioning**
Starting with product attributes instead of customer alternatives, value, and market. Leads to positioning that's internally focused (we have this feature!) rather than customer-focused (this matters to you because...).

**Investor Positioning vs. Customer Positioning**
Investors want to hear about your 5-10 year vision and market size. Customers want to know if you solve their problem today. These are different positioning narratives. Customer positioning belongs in your public materials, messaging, and sales.

**Positioning Drift**
When documentation exists but teams gradually reinterpret it differently, abandon it for convenience, or let it go stale as markets change. Prevented by regular review, enforcement, and updates.

## Market and Competitive Concepts

**Market Maturity**
The stage of market development. Early-stage markets (new problem, few solutions) require education positioning. Mature markets (established categories, many competitors) require differentiation positioning. Affects how you position.

**Market Category Leadership**
In mature categories, the leader (Salesforce in CRM, Slack in team chat) owns the category and sets assumptions. Challengers can fight the leader in the same category or reposition in a different category or subcategory where they have strength.

**Competitive Landscape**
All the ways customers could solve their problem (competitive alternatives). Includes direct competitors (other products), indirect alternatives (doing it manually, hiring), and status quo (doing nothing).

**Subcategory Positioning**
Instead of competing in a crowded category (e.g., "CRM"), position in a narrower subcategory where your strength is clearer ("CRM for real estate," "CRM for nonprofits"). Often easier than challenging the category leader.

**The Two Traps** (Dunford's framework)
1. **Trap 1:** You're stuck on what you intended to build; you don't realize the product has become something else (product changed, you didn't update positioning)
2. **Trap 2:** You positioned correctly for a market that's now changed; you're still using old positioning even though market has shifted

## Research and Validation Concepts

**Best-Fit Customers**
Your happiest, most loyal customers. They bought quickly, use heavily, refer you, and act as references. The foundation for positioning because they reveal what your product actually is and who truly values it.

**Heterogeneous vs. Homogeneous Customer Base**
Homogeneous: Best customers share clear characteristics; patterns are obvious; positioning is clear. Heterogeneous: No clear pattern; different customer types have different reasons for using you; true positioning hasn't emerged yet. Early-stage companies often have heterogeneous bases; more mature companies should trend toward homogeneous.

**Proof** (for value claims)
Customer testimonials, quantitative data, third-party validation (analyst reports, awards, G2 reviews), case studies, observable outcomes. Proof backs positioning claims. Opinion doesn't; only customer/expert/data-based evidence counts.

**Sales Signals**
Observable behaviors that indicate strong positioning: short sales cycles, high close rates, low price haggling, customer referrals, easy expansion to adjacent customers, high NPS scores. Weak positioning shows opposite signals: long cycles, low close rates, price pressure, no referrals, high churn.

## Organizational and Execution Concepts

**Positioning Team**
Cross-functional group (business leader, marketing, sales, product, customer success) responsible for developing and owning positioning. Must include business leader for credibility. Benefits from external facilitator to challenge assumptions and keep discussion balanced.

**Positioning Vocabulary**
Shared definitions of positioning terms so the team can discuss positioning without arguing about what words mean. Example: "By 'unique attributes,' we mean capabilities we have that competitive alternatives specifically lack—not generic superiority claims."

**Positioning Baggage Awareness**
The explicit step of surfacing how your history has shaped your current thinking about the product, then consciously setting that aside for the workshop so you can see alternatives.

**Buy-In Across Functions**
Positioning is only effective if every team (product, marketing, sales, success) understands it and executes against it. Requires visible business leader sponsorship, clear documentation, and enforcement.

**Positioning Documentation**
Written capture of all six components and how each function uses positioning. Prevents interpretation drift, makes onboarding easy, and serves as a reference for decisions. Living document, not static PDF.

## Strategic Concepts

**Positioning as Competitive Advantage**
Strong positioning is often the most sustainable advantage because it's hard to copy. Competitors can copy your features (attributes) but can't easily reposition against you if you've claimed the best category and audience.

**Positioning and Business Model**
Positioning shapes who you go to market through, what pricing model you can sustain, how you distribute. Example: "direct-to-consumer" positioning implies different channel strategy than "enterprise B2B."

**Positioning Flywheel**
Strong positioning → attracts right customers → they succeed with your product → they refer others like them → more customers from right segment → stronger data about what works → easier to market and sell → stronger positioning. Weak positioning reverses this.

**Market Expansion Through Repositioning**
You can enter new markets without changing your product by repositioning. Example: database company repositioning as data warehouse opened them to new customers, new geographies, new industry verticals—without touching the product.
