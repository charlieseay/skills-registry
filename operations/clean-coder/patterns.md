# Patterns — situational playbook

## You're pushed to commit to a date you're not sure of
→ State your real uncertainty as uncertainty ("probably, but it might be Monday"), not a soft yes. If forced to a binary answer, give the honest one even if it disappoints, per ch. 3.

## You're asked to "just try" to hit a tighter date
→ Recognize that agreeing to try IS agreeing to the tighter date (ch. 2, ch. 10). If you don't have a genuine reserve of effort and a real new plan, say so plainly instead of accepting the implied commitment.

## You're pressured to cut corners (skip tests, skip refactoring, skip the regression suite) to go faster
→ Refuse, and say why: skipping disciplines doesn't actually make you faster — it slows you down. This is the line professionals draw, per ch. 3 and ch. 11.

## An acceptance test, once you start implementing it, turns out to be wrong or awkward
→ Negotiate a better test with its author. Don't silently implement a test you believe is flawed just to avoid the conversation (ch. 7) — that's passive-aggressive, not professional.

## You realize you'll be late
→ Report best/nominal/worst-case dates continuously and honestly (ch. 4, ch. 10). Never let hope substitute for an honest number. Raise the flag as early as possible — early warning gives everyone room to adjust; late warning doesn't.

## You're deep in a stuck design and going back feels too expensive
→ Recognize the inflection point (ch. 9) — going back will never be cheaper than it is right now. Continuing forward through a known mess is "the worst kind of priority inversion."

## You catch yourself doing easier work to avoid harder, more important work
→ Name it as priority inversion (ch. 9) — a lie you're telling yourself, and rehearsal for a lie you'll tell someone else later. Reorder honestly.

## You're under real, unavoidable pressure (deadline slipping, design needs rework)
→ Don't rush — rushing produces worse output, not faster output. Communicate early (avoid surprises above all), lean HARDER into your normal disciplines (more tests, more refactoring, not less), and pair up rather than isolate (ch. 11).

## You're asked "how long will this take?"
→ Give a real distribution (optimistic/nominal/pessimistic or an explicit confidence level), not a bare number treated as a promise (ch. 10).

## A meeting invite has no clear agenda or goal
→ Decline, or ask for one before accepting (ch. 9). If a meeting you're in gets hijacked or becomes unproductive, politely ask if your presence is still needed and exit if not.

## A technical argument has gone on more than ~30 minutes without resolving
→ Per Kent Beck (ch. 9): "any argument that can't be settled in five minutes can't be settled by arguing." Go get data, or timebox each side to a short pitch and vote.

## You're deciding whether to work alone or pair on something
→ Default to pairing except for genuinely trivial tasks or tasks that need real solitary deep-think time (ch. 12). Pairing spreads knowledge, reviews code as it's written, and is usually the fastest path anyway (it's what people do in emergencies for a reason).

## You're staffing a new project
→ Prefer allocating it to an existing gelled team over assembling a fresh team around it (ch. 13) — fresh teams around each project never get time to gel.

## A junior developer joins your team
→ Treat early tenure as genuine apprenticeship: heavy pairing, close review, real teaching of TDD/refactoring/estimation — not just nominal supervision (ch. 14). Model the discipline you want them to adopt rather than lecturing about it.
