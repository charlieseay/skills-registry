# 11. Pressure

## The framing question

"Imagine you're the patient on the operating table during open-heart surgery, and the surgeon is under a real deadline. Do you want him calm, decisive, and adhering to his training — or sweating, swearing, throwing instruments, and blaming management?" **The professional developer is calm and decisive under pressure, and adheres MORE closely to discipline as pressure rises, not less.**

## Avoiding pressure (prevention, before it hits)

- **Commitments** — per Chapter 10, don't commit to deadlines you're not sure you can hit. When the business makes a promise on your behalf without consulting you, you're honor-bound to *help* find a way to meet it — but not honor-bound to *accept* it as your commitment. If it genuinely can't be met, responsibility sits with whoever made the promise, not with you for failing to achieve the impossible.
- **Staying clean** — "quick and dirty is an oxymoron. Dirty always means slow." Avoiding messes (see ch. 9) is itself a pressure-avoidance strategy, since messes are what generate the crunches in the first place.
- **Crisis discipline (the self-test)** — "You know what you believe by observing yourself in a crisis." If you follow TDD normally but drop it under pressure, you don't actually believe TDD helps. If you keep code clean normally but make messes in a crisis, you don't actually believe messes slow you down. The prescription: **choose disciplines you're willing to follow even in a crisis, then actually follow them all the time** — consistency under pressure is the real test of whether a discipline is genuinely load-bearing or just a normal-times nicety.

## Handling pressure (once it's already arrived)

1. **Don't panic.** Sleepless nights and fretting don't produce faster output. **Rushing is explicitly named as the worst response** — it drives you deeper into the hole, not out of it. Slow down, think the problem through, then move at a steady, sustainable pace toward the best achievable outcome.
2. **Communicate.** Tell your team and superiors you're in trouble, share your plan for getting out, ask for input. **Avoid surprises above all** — "nothing makes people more angry and less rational than surprises. Surprises multiply the pressure by ten."
3. **Rely on your disciplines, harder than usual.** This is not the moment to question or loosen TDD, refactoring, or small functions — it's the moment to lean into them more: write *more* tests than usual, refactor *more* mercilessly, keep functions *even* smaller. Panicked improvisation is the wrong instinct; doubling down on what already reliably works is the right one.
4. **Get help — pair.** Pairing under pressure gets things done faster with fewer defects, and a partner helps you actually hold to your disciplines instead of panicking alone. The same obligation runs the other way: when you see a teammate under pressure, **offer to pair with them.**

## Build your new habit

- **WHEN THIS HAPPENS...** A project hits real, unavoidable pressure (a slipped deadline, a design that has to be reworked, a lost team member).
- **INSTEAD OF...** Rushing, dropping your normal disciplines "just this once," or going quiet and hoping to surprise everyone with a last-minute save.
- **I WILL...** Slow down and think, communicate the real situation early (no surprises), lean harder into my normal disciplines rather than abandoning them, and pair up rather than isolate.
