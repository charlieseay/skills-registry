# 9. Time Management

## Meetings — attend deliberately, not by default

Meetings are simultaneously **necessary** and **huge time-wasters**, often the same meeting for different attendees. Estimated real cost: ~$200/hour per attendee once salary, benefits, and facilities are counted.

- **Declining:** you're not obligated to attend every meeting you're invited to — "it is unprofessional to go to too many meetings." Accept only when your presence is immediately and significantly necessary to your current work. Weigh authority/interest against your actual project responsibility, ideally with your manager's input. **A good manager's job includes keeping you out of meetings**, and will defend that decision.
- **Leaving:** if a meeting turns out to be a bad use of your time (scope creep, hijacked agenda, no longer needs you), it's *unprofessional to stay silently* — politely ask if your presence is still needed and exit if not. Rude exits aren't required; silent, wasted attendance isn't professional either.
- **Have an agenda and a goal, always.** If you can't get a clear agenda/timebox/goal before a meeting, decline. If the agenda gets hijacked mid-meeting, ask to table the new topic and return to plan, or leave.

### Standard meeting shapes and their time budgets
- **Stand-ups:** 3 questions per person (What did I do yesterday? What will I do today? What's in my way?), ~20 sec/question, whole meeting under 10 minutes even for a 10-person team.
- **Iteration planning:** prep work (estimates, business value, ideally sketched tests) should already be done before the meeting; 5-10 min per backlog item max; rule of thumb, the whole meeting ≤5% of the iteration's total time (2 hours for a 1-week iteration).
- **Retro + demo:** schedule near the end of the last day, ~20 min retro + ~25 min demo — it's only been a week or two, there shouldn't be much to say.

### Settling arguments
Kent Beck: *"Any argument that can't be settled in five minutes can't be settled by arguing."* Beyond that window it's not a factual disagreement anymore, it's a values/preference clash — force of character (yelling, condescension) doesn't resolve it, and passive-aggressive fake-agreement ("fine, do it your way" then quietly sabotaging) is called out as **the worst kind of unprofessional behavior** — if you agree, you must actually engage. Get real data (experiment, simulation) or, when data isn't feasible, agree explicitly on a path, a time bound, and abandonment criteria — and if truly stuck, timebox each side to a 5-minute pitch and vote.

## Focus-Manna — treat concentration as a finite, rechargeable resource

A deliberately informal framing: focus is a scarce, decaying resource. Spend it in meetings or on worry and you have less for coding; force code output when it's depleted and you'll be rewriting it later. Levers that affect it:

- **Sleep** — the single strongest lever named ("I can't stress this one strongly enough"). A good night's sleep reliably restores a full day's focus capacity.
- **Caffeine** — helps in moderation but can add a distracting "jitter" that misdirects focus if overused; personal tolerance varies.
- **Recharging** — de-focus deliberately: a walk, a real conversation, staring out a window, meditation, a nap. Once focus-manna is genuinely gone, forcing more code just creates rework later — better to take 30-60 minutes to actually recover.
- **Muscle focus** — physical disciplines (martial arts, tai-chi, yoga, cycling, any hands-on hobby) use a *different* kind of focus that seems to recharge — and over time increase capacity for — mental focus.
- **Input vs. output** — creative output (code) is fed by creative input (for the author, science fiction) — same idea as Chapter 4's "creative input" fix for writer's block.

## Timeboxing — the Pomodoro Technique

25-minute focused blocks ("tomatoes"), all interruptions deferred to the timer's end ("can I call you back in 25 minutes?"), a ~5-minute break after each, a longer ~30-minute break every 4th tomato. Only the time *inside* tomatoes counts as truly productive time — counting your daily tomato count gives a fast, honest read on how much of the day is actually being spent working vs. absorbed by "stuff."

## Avoidance and priority inversion

**Priority inversion:** raising a lower-priority task's apparent urgency so you can avoid a scarier/harder/higher-priority one. Named explicitly as **a lie you tell yourself** — and secretly, preparation for the lie you'll tell someone who asks what you're working on. Professionals evaluate priority honestly regardless of personal fear or preference and work in that real order.

## Blind alleys — "The Rule of Holes: when you're in one, stop digging"

A technical dead end is unavoidable eventually — the risk isn't hitting one, it's how invested you get before admitting it. The more personal/reputational stake in a decision, the longer people wander before backing out (sometimes "forever" if reputation is fully staked). The actual skill: **recognize the dead end quickly and have the courage to reverse**, keeping other options open rather than becoming so vested in one path you can't leave it.

## Messes — worse than blind alleys

A blind alley stops you; a **mess** just slows you while still *looking* like forward progress is possible — which is exactly why it's more dangerous. The progression is gradual: a clean solution to a simple problem, extended as scope grows, until a wrong early design choice becomes visible — that's **the inflection point**. Going back looks expensive (rework) but "going back will never be easier than it is now." Continuing forward once you *know* it's a mess is called "the worst kind of priority inversion" — you're lying to yourself, your team, and your customers about where things are actually headed. "Nothing has a more profound or long-lasting negative effect on the productivity of a software team than a mess. Nothing."

## Build your new habit

- **WHEN THIS HAPPENS...** You're invited to a meeting without a clear agenda/goal, or you catch yourself doing a lower-priority task to avoid a harder one, or a design decision starts to feel like it's turning into a mess.
- **INSTEAD OF...** Accepting every invite by default, rationalizing the avoidance as legitimate reprioritization, or pushing forward through a recognized mess because turning back looks expensive.
- **I WILL...** Decline meetings without a stated agenda/timebox/goal, name priority inversion when I catch myself doing it, and treat the moment I recognize a mess as the cheapest time I'll ever have to turn back.
