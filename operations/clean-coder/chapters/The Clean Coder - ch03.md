# 3. Saying Yes

## Core claim

Saying no is only half the discipline. **When you say yes, use a language of commitment that makes it unambiguous — for you and for whoever's counting on you.**

## A Language of Commitment (Roy Osherove, guest section)

**Say. Mean. Do.** Real commitment has three stages — most people stall at stage 1 or 2.

### Telltale phrases of *non*-commitment
- **"Need / should"** — "We need to get this done," "someone should make that happen" — passive, no owner.
- **"Hope / wish"** — "I hope to get this done by tomorrow" — an aspiration, not a promise.
- **"Let's..."** (not followed by "I...") — "Let's finish this thing" — diffuses ownership across a group of nobody.

All three share the same tell: the speaker is behaving like a **victim of the situation**, not someone in control of it.

### What real commitment sounds like
The formula: **"I will [X] by [date]."** A factual, first-person, binary statement — no "possibly," no "try to." Once said, there are exactly two outcomes: you did it, or you didn't. That's uncomfortable by design; the discomfort *is* the commitment.

### Handling the three excuses for not committing

| Excuse | Reframe |
|---|---|
| "It depends on someone else" | Commit to a specific *action under your control* that moves toward the goal (e.g., "I will sit down with Gary for an hour," not "I will finish the integrated module"). |
| "I don't know if it's even possible" | Commit to finding out — that itself is a valid, concrete commitment (e.g., "I will reproduce all 25 bugs by Thursday"). |
| "Sometimes I just won't make it" | Raise the red flag **as early as possible**, to everyone who's counting on the commitment — early warning gives the team room to actually adjust; a late warning doesn't. |

## Learning How to Say Yes

### The other side of "try"

A developer giving fuzzy answers ("I think that's doable," "I'll try to get that done") to a manager's direct yes/no question is dodging commitment, not making one. The fix isn't blunt refusal — it's **honest uncertainty stated as uncertainty**: "Probably, but it might be Monday" is more useful and more professional than a hollow "yes."

### Committing with discipline — full worked dialogue

When pushed for a definite answer under real pressure (a hard external deadline depending on the developer's output), the professional move, in order:

1. **State the true, disciplined estimate as fact**, even if it disappoints: "No, the soonest I can be sure is Tuesday."
2. If the requester pushes for an earlier date by asking you to cut corners (skip tests, skip refactoring, skip the regression suite) — **refuse, explicitly, and explain why the shortcut won't even work**: skipping tests/refactoring/regression doesn't make you faster, "years of experience have taught us that breaking disciplines only slows us down." Professional standards (tests, clean code, verified no regressions) are a *prior* commitment that all new commitments are subordinate to.
3. If there is real, honest slack available (energy, family bandwidth, a genuinely feasible overtime plan) — **offer it explicitly, with a real boundary attached**: "I'll clear it with my family, work through the weekend, be here Monday to help — then I'm out until Wednesday. Deal?"

Professionals know their limits and state overtime as a bounded, deliberate trade — not an open-ended sacrifice.

## Build your new habit

- **WHEN THIS HAPPENS...** You're asked for a commitment (a deadline, a deliverable) by a manager, client, or teammate.
- **INSTEAD OF...** Using "need/should," "hope/wish," or unowned "let's" language that leaves the commitment fuzzy and unaccountable.
- **I WILL...** State it as "I will [X] by [date]" — a first-person, binary, factual promise — or state my genuine uncertainty as uncertainty rather than disguise it as a soft yes.
