# 2. Saying No

## Core claim

**"Professionals speak truth to power. Professionals have the courage to say no to their managers."** Good managers *crave* someone who has the guts to say no — it's the only way to get anything real done. Slaves can't say no; laborers hesitate to say no; professionals are expected to say no.

## Adversarial roles are healthy, not a failure

Managers are doing their job when they aggressively pursue their objectives. Programmers are doing *their* job only if they defend their own objectives (accurate estimates, sound engineering) just as aggressively. Avoiding all confrontation is not harmony — it's usually one side quietly failing to do their job.

### Three example negotiations (same request, three outcomes)

1. **Avoidant / dishonest:** Manager asks for an impossible deadline, developer says "I'll try," manager hears "Yes." No confrontation, but a lie was told and heard as a commitment.
2. **Passive:** Developer states the real timeline as a question ("How about two weeks?") instead of a fact; manager just accepts it without pushback. Both sides fail to actually negotiate toward the true best outcome.
3. **Adversarial-but-professional (the model):** Developer states the fact plainly ("No, that's a two-week job"), manager pushes back with real numbers, developer holds the line but offers a real, scoped alternative (a working mock-up minus specific named features). Confrontation happened, but it converged on a genuine, deliverable commitment both sides understand.

**On explaining "why":** the *fact* ("it will take two weeks") matters more than the *why*. Too much justifying detail invites the other person to second-guess your process and micromanage individual steps.

## High stakes make "no" more valuable, not less

The higher the cost of being wrong, the more essential an honest "no" becomes — this is exactly when the temptation to just say "we'll try" is strongest, and exactly when it's most damaging.

## Being a "Team Player" ≠ always saying yes

A team player represents what can and can't actually be done, to the best of their ability — even under pressure to say otherwise. In the book's extended example, the developer who repeatedly says "no, it's eight or nine weeks, not six" *is* the real team player; the manager who tells his boss "we'll make it happen" while privately squeezing the team through overtime is "playing on a team of one."

## "Trying" is a trap — say what you'll actually do instead

**"There is no trying"** (channeling Yoda, deliberately). To promise you'll *try* implies you were holding back effort in reserve, and that you have a *new plan* to unlock it. If you don't actually have a new plan or hidden reserve, "I'll try" is dishonest — a way to avoid confrontation while still setting yourself up to be blamed for "not trying hard enough."

**Better move:** restate the estimate as a fact, refuse to imply flexibility that doesn't exist, and if pressured further, respond concretely: "I could try to levitate. Do you think I'd succeed?"

## Passive aggression vs. direct escalation

When a manager is hiding bad news from *their* boss (letting a deadline slide silently rather than escalating), you have two choices: let them walk off the cliff (passive-aggressive — technically covered, ethically weak) or **force the real conversation** directly and on the record. The book favors escalating directly, in writing, with a clear deadline for the manager to do it themselves first.

## The Cost of Saying Yes — case study ("Is Good Code Impossible?")

A real blog post (John Blanco) about a 2-week iPhone app deadline that ballooned (scope crept via new "web service" requirements that didn't exist, email registration, coupon expiration) while the client kept adding features and pushing dates. The developer worked 90-hour weeks, skipped unit tests, used copy-paste and magic numbers, shipped broken/incomplete work, and the app never actually launched.

**The formula for scope creep, as satire:**
```
(# of Executives)² + 2×(# of New Executives) + (# of Bob's Kids) = DAYS ADDED AT LAST MINUTE
```

**Who's actually at fault?** Not the client — asking for things and paying for options is a client's right. **The developer.** He said yes to the original deadline knowing it was unrealistic, said yes to every added feature, and — critically — said yes to *dropping his own professional disciplines* (no tests, no design, "there's no time"). Dropping disciplines under pressure doesn't solve schedule problems, it creates worse ones.

**"Is good code impossible?"** — the book's direct answer: **No.** The failure was saying yes when the professional answer was no, at multiple points along the way — not just to the deadline, but to which shortcuts to allow.

## Build your new habit

- **WHEN THIS HAPPENS...** A manager or client asks for a deadline/scope you know is unrealistic, or pressures you to "just try."
- **INSTEAD OF...** Saying "I'll try" (a hidden lie if you have no real reserve or new plan), or silently accepting an estimate you know is wrong.
- **I WILL...** State the real estimate as a fact, hold the line under pressure, and offer a genuinely deliverable alternative (reduced scope, more time) instead of a false yes.
