# 6. Practicing

## Core claim

All professionals practice outside of performance — musicians rehearse scales, football players run drills, doctors practice sutures, lawyers rehearse arguments. **Programming is unusual among professions in that programmers historically didn't practice this way**, largely because early programming (slow compiles, no screen editors) didn't reward fast reflexes. Modern tooling (fast builds, tight TDD loops) changed that.

## Kata — solo practice

A **kata** is a precisely choreographed sequence of keystrokes that simulates solving a known problem. You already know the solution — the point isn't solving it, it's training your fingers and reflexes to execute the *movements* of solving it until they become automatic, the way a martial artist drills a kata to make combat responses instinctive.

Named examples: **The Bowling Game**, **Prime Factors**, **Word Wrap** (all findable at katas.softwarecraftsmanship.org / codekata.pragprog.com).

**Why bother:** at high tempo (the book cites spinning the compile/test loop up to 10x/minute on a well-tuned project), you don't have time to consciously deliberate — your body/fingers need to already know common moves so your mind is free for higher-level design thinking. Analogy: a skilled guitarist doesn't think about finger placement; a martial artist in combat doesn't pause to plan a block.

## Two-person and group practice

- **Wasa** (from jujitsu) → **ping-pong pairing**: one partner writes a failing test, the other makes it pass, then they swap. Choosing a *new* problem (not a known kata) makes this genuinely competitive — the test-writer can constrain the solution space (e.g., speed/memory limits) to challenge their partner.
- **Randori**: group version. One person writes a test on a shared screen and sits down; the next person makes it pass and writes the next test; repeat around the room. High-value for seeing many different people's problem-solving approaches in one session.

## Broadening your experience

- Employers often force narrow language/platform/domain exposure — left unchecked, this narrows your resume and mindset, and leaves you unprepared when the industry shifts.
- **Contribute to open source** as your "pro bono" work (the explicit analogy: like lawyers/doctors doing pro bono) — if you're a Java developer at work, contribute to a Rails or Python project on your own time.

## Practice ethics

**Practice happens on your own time, not your employer's** — same "not your employer's job" framing as Chapter 1's work-ethic argument. Patients don't pay doctors to practice sutures; employers don't owe you paid practice time. Since it's your own time, you're also free to practice in *different* languages/stacks than your day job uses — deliberately broadening rather than just deepening the same skill.

## Build your new habit

- **WHEN THIS HAPPENS...** You have unstructured personal learning time, or you notice your reflexes/toolchain fluency have gotten rusty.
- **INSTEAD OF...** Only ever coding "for real" on the job, in the same language/stack every time.
- **I WILL...** Run a kata (Bowling Game, Prime Factors, etc.) as a short warm-up/cool-down, and periodically practice in a language or domain outside my day job.
