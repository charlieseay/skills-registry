# 12. Collaboration

## The uncomfortable premise

Programmers, as a group, are stereotyped (with the book's own caveat that it's a broad generalization with many real exceptions) as more drawn to the clean predictability of machines than to messy interpersonal work. **The book's answer to this isn't to fight the stereotype — it's to say the field requires collaboration regardless, so build the disciplines that force it.**

## Programmers vs. employers — care about the business, not just the puzzle

A personal story: solving a hard bug felt like "slaying the Jabberwock," and the author would excitedly recount the elegance of a bug to his boss — who eventually snapped, "Bugs aren't interesting. Bugs just need to be fixed!" The lesson drawn: passion for the craft is good, but **your first responsibility is your employer's actual goals**, not the technical puzzle for its own sake. Professionals talk to users, sales, and marketing to understand why they're building what they're building — "the worst thing a professional can do is blissfully bury himself in a tomb of technology while the business crashes and burns around him."

A cautionary personal anecdote follows: the author was fired at 24 for treating attendance, punctuality, and organizational norms as beneath his notice because he was "the best technical programmer on the team" — the point being that technical excellence doesn't exempt you from the collaborative, organizational side of the job.

## Programmers vs. programmers — code ownership

- **Owned code (anti-pattern):** individual programmers wall off "their" modules and refuse to let others touch or even see the code — the book's real example is a company where component importance (feeder vs. stapler vs. printer) directly mapped to a programmer's political leverage, producing massive duplication and mismatched interfaces that no argument could dislodge because salaries were tied to guarding your turf.
- **Collective ownership (the fix):** any team member can check out and modify any module. The team owns the code, not individuals. This isn't just about code quality — it's explicitly framed as anti-turf-building.

## Pairing, again, with new framing

Programmers who dislike pairing routinely *do* pair in emergencies — because it's obviously the fastest way to solve a hard problem under pressure. The book's question: **if pairing is the most efficient approach in an emergency, why treat it as optional the rest of the time?** Three stated reasons professionals pair regularly, not just in crises:
1. It's often the most efficient way to solve the problem, period.
2. It's the best way to **spread knowledge** and avoid knowledge silos — everyone should be able to "play another position in a pinch."
3. It's the most efficient form of **code review** — reviewing-while-writing beats most after-the-fact review processes.

## "Cerebellums" — a satirical aside with a real point

A recruiting billboard invited programmers to "come rub cerebellums with the best" — the cerebellum handles fine motor control, not intelligence, and it sits at the *back* of the skull, so "rubbing cerebellums" would literally mean sitting back-to-back, alone, facing away from each other — which the author points out is exactly how isolated cubicle culture (headphones on, backs turned) actually works, and exactly the opposite of collaboration. **"That's how you rub cerebellums. That's also not a team."** The prescription: sit facing each other, at shared tables, where informal/serendipitous communication (verbal and body language) can happen — while still allowing for legitimate solo-focus time on genuinely solitary or trivial tasks.

## Build your new habit

- **WHEN THIS HAPPENS...** You're deep in a technically interesting problem, or you're territorial about "your" module, or you're deciding whether to work alone or pair.
- **INSTEAD OF...** Losing sight of why the business cares about the problem, walling off code from teammates, or defaulting to solo work out of habit.
- **I WILL...** Connect the technical work back to the actual business goal, treat all code as team-owned, and default to pairing except when a task is genuinely trivial or requires focused solitary thought.
