# 7. The Learning Question — "What was most useful for you?"

## Why people don't retain what they're told

Chris Argyris's "double-loop learning": the first loop fixes a problem; the second loop creates a learning moment *about* the problem. People don't really learn by being told, or even by doing — they learn when they get a chance to **recall and reflect** on what just happened. This question forces that second loop.

## The AGES model (NeuroLeadership Institute) for durable memory

Four drivers of longer-term retention: **Attention, Generation, Emotion, Spacing.** The relevant one here is **Generation** — creating your own connection to an idea (vs. passively receiving it) substantially increases retention. Telling someone something has limited odds of reaching the hippocampus; asking them to generate the answer increases those odds.

## "To learn, retrieve" (Make It Stick — Brown, Roediger, McDaniel)

Forgetting starts immediately after a conversation ends. Asking a reflection question — even just at the end — is "the first interruption" in that forgetting curve. Reflection itself is a form of practice.

## Why this exact phrasing beats the alternatives

Compared to "What did you learn?" or "What was the key insight?", **"What was most useful for you?"** does six things at once:

1. Assumes the conversation *was* useful (frames it as worth mining for value)
2. Asks for the One Big Thing, not an overwhelming list
3. "For you" makes it personal, not abstract
4. Gives *you* feedback on what actually landed
5. It's learning, not judgment — "Was this useful?" (Yes/No) invites a verdict; "What was most useful?" forces extraction of value
6. Reminds the person, subtly, that the conversation with you was worth having

## The Coaching Bookends

Pair the **Kickstart Question** (open) with the **Learning Question** (close) — even without the other five questions, this pair alone reshapes how conversations start and end.

## The peak-end rule

Kahneman's research (including, memorably, on colonoscopies): how people remember an experience is disproportionately shaped by its peak and its *ending*. Finishing a conversation on "this was useful" makes the whole conversation retroactively feel more valuable — independent of how it went in the middle.

## Build your new habit

- **WHEN THIS HAPPENS...** A conversation or meeting is wrapping up — in person, virtual, after a 1:1, after a speech, with a customer or colleague.
- **INSTEAD OF...** Just checking the action list, rushing to the next meeting, or telling them how great they did without extracting the actual insight.
- **I WILL...** Ask, "What was most useful here for you?" (or "What did you find most valuable about this chat?")
