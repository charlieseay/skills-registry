# 3. The Focus Question — "What's the real challenge here for you?"

## The problem it solves

When someone raises a challenge, it's rarely the *actual* problem — it can be a symptom, a secondary issue, or a half-baked solution to something unarticulated. Jumping to fix the first-named problem means: you solve the wrong problem, you do work your team should do, and the real work doesn't get done.

## Why it's phrased exactly this way

The question builds in three stages:

1. *"What's the challenge?"* — too vague, generates an obvious or abstract answer.
2. *"What's the **real** challenge here?"* — implies there are several to choose from; slows people down.
3. *"What's the real challenge here **for you**?"* — the "for you" pins it to the person in front of you, not an abstract situation.

## The three Foggy-fiers (patterns that keep things vague)

| Foggy-fier | Symptom | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Proliferation of Challenges** | A flood of unrelated problems (website project, a colleague, budget anxiety, a noisy car engine — all in one breath) | "If you had to pick one of these to focus on, which one here would be the real challenge for you?" |
| **Coaching the Ghost** | Talking at length about a third party (a difficult coworker, a project, a situation) rather than themselves | "I think I understand some of what's going on with [X]. What's the real challenge here for you?" |
| **Abstractions & Generalizations** | High-level, academic, "us/we" talk with no "me/I" | "I have a sense of the overall challenge. What's the real challenge here for you?" |

## Coaching for development, not just performance

Adding "for you" nudges every question from performance-coaching (solve the problem) toward development-coaching (grow the person solving it). A 1997 study on math word problems found that inserting "you" into the problem statement measurably improved speed and accuracy — specificity to the person sharpens thinking.

## Three strategies to make it work

1. **Trust that you're being useful** even when you're "just asking questions" — watch for the pause where you can see them actually thinking.
2. **Remember there's a place for advice** — if someone just needs to know where the folder is, tell them. Don't over-apply the question.
3. **Stack it with the AWE Question** — "What's the real challenge here for you?" then "And what else is a real challenge here for you?"

## Build your new habit

- **WHEN THIS HAPPENS...** The team is discussing a challenge or project and has already jumped to solutions; someone's wrestling with a problem but you're not sure they know what it actually is; you're anxious or uncertain about a challenge yourself.
- **INSTEAD OF...** Assuming the first-named challenge is the real one; keeping it abstract; trying to fix all of the listed problems at once; treating someone else's problem as the topic; skipping straight to action.
- **I WILL...** Ask, "What's the real challenge here for you?"
