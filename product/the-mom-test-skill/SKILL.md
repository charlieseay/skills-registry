## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**The Mom Test** (Ch 1) — three rules for questions even your mom can't lie to you about: (1) talk about their life instead of your idea, (2) ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future, (3) talk less and listen more. Never ask anyone whether your idea is good — only the market can answer that.

**Three types of bad data, three fixes** (Ch 2):
- *Compliments* → **deflect** — redirect back to facts about their life; a compliment costs the speaker nothing, so it carries zero data.
- *Fluff* (generics, future-tense, hypotheticals — "I usually," "I would," "I might") → **anchor** — ask "when's the last time that happened?" then "walk me through it."
- *Ideas / feature requests / emotional signals* → **dig** — ask "why do you want that?", "what would that let you do?" before adding anything to a roadmap.

**Important questions** (Ch 3) — every conversation should include at least one question whose answer could completely change or disprove your business. Distinguish **product risk** (can I build/grow it?) from **market risk** (do they want/pay for it?) — conversations only validate market risk. **Look before you zoom**: start with broad goal/problem questions before narrowing to your specific angle, or you'll manufacture false validation. A lukewarm "meh" is more reliable data than an enthusiastic "wow!" — never try to upgrade a lukewarm response with more pitching.

**Keeping it casual** (Ch 4) — skip the formal meeting where possible; transition into your most important question wherever you already are (conferences, cafes, meetups). The Meeting Anti-Pattern is relegating every learning opportunity to a scheduled calendar block, which loses both time and serendipity.

**Commitment and Advancement** (Ch 5) — once you have a product to show, every meeting must end in either advancement (a real next step) or rejection — anything else is a zombie lead. Weight signals by currency given up: cash > reputation risk > time > compliment (zero). "It's not a real lead until you've given them a concrete chance to reject you."

**Finding conversations** (Ch 6) — the goal of cold outreach is to stop needing it, by converting every cold conversation into warm intros. Better still, "bring them to you" via meetups, teaching, or blogging. Use **VFWPA** (Vision / Framing / Weakness / Pedestal / Ask) to request meetings without accidentally pitching.

**Customer Slicing** (Ch 7) — a vague segment ("students," "advertisers") is actually many segments in disguise, producing inconsistent feedback. Repeatedly narrow: which sub-type wants it most? why (motivation)? who else shares that motivation? where can they be found? Stop once you have a specific, reachable **who-where pair**. Pick a first segment for being profitable, easy to reach, and personally rewarding.

**Running the process** (Ch 8) — avoid the **learning bottleneck** (one founder hoarding all customer insight) with a repeatable Prep → Conversation → Review cycle done as a team. The single most important prep question: "What do we want to learn from these guys?" Use lightweight, symbol-coded notes (index cards work well) so learning is shareable, not trapped in one person's head.
