# Chapter 13. Business Entity Selection and Taxation

## Overview of Business Entity Options

Business owners face multiple entity structure choices, each with tax, legal, and operational implications. The optimal choice depends on business type, owner situation, profitability, and complexity.

## Sole Proprietorship

### Structure
- Individual business owner with no separate entity
- No formal business formation required
- Simplest business structure

### Tax Treatment
- Business income/loss reported on Schedule C (individual return)
- Taxed at individual marginal rates
- No separate entity-level tax

### Self-Employment Tax
- 15.3% tax on net self-employment income (Social Security and Medicare)
- Calculated on Schedule SE
- No opportunity to split income/employment tax
- Growing source of tax burden for successful sole proprietorships

### Liability
- Personal liability for business obligations
- Business creditors can pursue personal assets
- Professional malpractice exposure
- Home, savings, vehicles at risk

### Advantages
- Simplicity in formation and operation
- Minimal compliance requirements
- No separate tax returns
- Flexibility in decision-making

### Disadvantages
- Personal liability exposure
- Self-employment tax on all net income
- Difficult succession if owner dies/becomes disabled
- Less professional appearance
- Difficulty obtaining financing

### Best For
- Small, low-risk businesses
- Service businesses with minimal assets
- Businesses operated part-time
- Businesses where liability not major concern

## Partnership

### Structure
- Two or more individuals sharing ownership
- Can be general (GP) or limited (LP) partnership
- Requires written partnership agreement

### General Partnership (GP)
- All partners participate in management
- Each partner personally liable for partnership obligations
- Pass-through taxation (profits taxed at individual level)

### Limited Partnership (LP)
- General partner(s) manage business
- Limited partners invest and receive distributions (passive investors)
- Limited partners' liability capped at investment
- General partner(s) still have personal liability

### Tax Treatment
- Partnership files Form 1065 (informational return)
- Profits/losses "pass through" to partner returns
- Each partner reports share on individual returns
- Partnership itself does not pay income tax

### Self-Employment Tax
- General partners pay self-employment tax on entire share
- Limited partners may avoid if truly passive
- Reasonableness test applied to partnership allocation

### Advantages
- Pass-through taxation (avoiding entity-level tax)
- Limited partner liability protection (for limited partners)
- Partnership agreement flexibility
- Shared management and resources

### Disadvantages
- General partners have personal liability
- Dissolved if partner withdraws (unless structured otherwise)
- Complexity if partners disagree
- Difficulty obtaining loans
- Double taxation if converted to C-corp

### Best For
- Professional firms (law, accounting, medical)
- Investment partnerships
- Multiple owner businesses
- Pass-through tax structure desired

## Limited Liability Company (LLC)

### Structure
- Pass-through entity with liability protection
- Can be single-member or multi-member
- Operates under LLC agreement
- Filing required in formation state

### Liability Protection
- Owner(s) liable only up to investment
- Business creditors cannot pursue personal assets (in most states)
- Professional liability exceptions apply (doctors, lawyers)

### Tax Treatment
- Default: Treated as sole proprietorship (single-member) or partnership (multi-member)
- Election option: Taxed as corporation (rare)
- Pass-through taxation with liability protection

### Self-Employment Tax
- Multi-member: Same as partnership
- Single-member: Same as sole proprietorship
- Can reduce through reasonable salary/distribution split

### Advantages
- Liability protection without corporate formality
- Pass-through taxation
- Flexible profit-sharing arrangements
- Simple formation
- Pass-through election option available

### Disadvantages
- Formation costs and annual filings required
- Self-employment tax on all income (unless S-corp election)
- Annual report requirements vary by state
- Dissolution upon member withdrawal (unless structured otherwise)
- Less well-established than corporations

### Best For
- Real estate investing
- Professional practices (in states allowing)
- Small to medium businesses
- Businesses where liability protection important but tax flexibility desired

## S-Corporation

### Structure
- Corporation (separate legal entity) with tax election (Form 2553)
- Can have up to 100 shareholders
- All shareholders must be U.S. individuals
- Single class of stock
- Requires articles of incorporation and bylaws

### Significant Self-Employment Tax Planning Opportunity
**S-Corp Advantage: Income Splitting**
- Owner receives reasonable salary (W-2 income subject to employment tax)
- Remaining profits distributed as dividends (not subject to self-employment tax)
- Requires payroll system
- IRS scrutinizes if salary unreasonably low

**Example Impact:**
- $100,000 net business income
- Sole prop: 15.3% SE tax = $15,300
- S-corp: $60,000 salary + $40,000 dividend
  - Salary: Withheld for Social Security/Medicare
  - Dividend: No additional self-employment tax
  - Significant tax savings if income substantial

### Tax Treatment
- Profits/losses flow through to shareholder returns
- No entity-level income tax
- Shareholders report pro-rata share on personal returns

### Advantages
- Liability protection (corporate form)
- Pass-through taxation
- Self-employment tax reduction on distributions
- Well-established entity type
- Facilitates capital contributions

### Disadvantages
- Formation and ongoing compliance costs
- Payroll required (even for single owner)
- Quarterly tax deposits and filings
- Termination of S-corp status loses tax benefits
- Shareholder ownership restrictions (U.S. individuals only)
- More complex accounting and tax filings

### Best For
- Profitable businesses ($60,000+ annual income)
- Business owners wanting liability protection and tax optimization
- Pass-through taxation desired
- Service businesses (consulting, professional services)
- Businesses with significant owner income

## C-Corporation

### Structure
- Standard corporate entity
- Separate legal person
- Unlimited shareholders
- Multiple classes of stock possible
- Requires articles of incorporation, bylaws, board of directors

### Taxation: Double Taxation Risk
- Corporation pays tax on profits at corporate rate (21%)
- Shareholders pay tax on dividends at personal rates (15% or 20%)
- Economic double taxation on profits distributed

### Income Tax Advantages (Limited)
- Ability to retain earnings (some tax deferral)
- Multiple tax brackets before reaching top rate
- Deductible corporate expenses
- Losses remain in corporation (can't pass to shareholders)

### Tax Disadvantages
- Double taxation on distributions
- Capital gains retained in corporation taxed twice
- Loss of corporate losses (trapped in entity)
- More complex accounting

### Advantages
- Liability protection
- Multiple classes of stock
- Unlimited shareholders
- Easier capital raising
- Professional appearance

### Disadvantages
- Double taxation on distributions
- Compliance costs and complexity
- Separate tax return requirements
- Stated capital formalities
- Generally disadvantageous for pass-through business income

### Best For
- Publicly traded corporations
- Businesses planning significant retained earnings reinvestment
- Businesses that will be sold (can minimize shareholder tax)
- Some professional corporations (tax limitations)

## Entity Election Decision Framework

### Step 1: Liability Assessment
- Risk of personal liability
- Type of assets/business
- Need for liability protection

### Step 2: Profitability Analysis
- Current and expected income level
- Impact of self-employment tax (for sole prop/single-member LLC)
- S-corp tax savings if profitable

### Step 3: Complexity Tolerance
- Willingness to maintain formalities
- Accounting/compliance cost acceptable
- Administrative burden tolerance

### Step 4: Tax Optimization
- Current marginal tax rate
- Expected future rates
- Self-employment tax impact
- Comparative calculations

### Step 5: Flexibility Needs
- Future growth and capital needs
- Potential ownership changes
- Financing/investor requirements
- Exit strategy impact

## Converting Between Entity Types

### Sole Proprietorship to LLC/S-Corp
- Form new entity and transfer assets
- No tax on entity formation (generally)
- Potential sales tax on asset transfer (state-dependent)
- Continue business operations

### LLC to S-Corporation
- File IRS Form 2553 (S-corp election)
- May require state filings
- Maintained liability protection
- Changes tax treatment immediately

### Partnership to LLC
- More complex if multiple partners
- New entity formation and asset transfer
- Potential step-up in basis issues
- Partnership termination for tax purposes

### Timing Considerations
- Mid-year elections have complications
- Coordination with tax year end
- Employment tax filing changes
- Updated compliance calendars required

## Professional Service Corporations

### Special Considerations
- Licensing requirements vary by profession
- Professional liability cannot be limited through LLC/corporation
- Some states restrict entity types
- Malpractice insurance requirements

### Recommended Structures
- Professional LLC (where available)
- Professional Corporation (PC/PA)
- Shareholder liability limited to personal malpractice (not other shareholders')

## Chapter Summary

Business entity selection significantly impacts tax burden, liability exposure, and operational complexity. Sole proprietorships offer simplicity but expose personal assets to business liability and all income to self-employment tax. Partnerships and LLCs provide liability protection with pass-through taxation but require more compliance. S-corporations add significant self-employment tax savings for profitable businesses. C-corporations create double taxation and are rarely optimal for pass-through income. The optimal entity depends on profitability level, liability exposure, complexity tolerance, and tax rate expectations. Conversion between entities is possible but requires careful tax planning.
